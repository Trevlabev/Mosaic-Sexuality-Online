SCOPE

Structure, Consent, Openness, Power, and Entanglement

A multilevel relationship-ecology framework for describing how consensual nonmonogamy actually operates: who is connected, what is open, how agreements are governed, where power resides, and how relationships become emotionally or materially intertwined.

Seven relational states

What someone calls, wants, agrees to, does, experiences, can sustain, and is constrained from doing may all differ.

SCOPE avoids treating those differences as contradictions. It maps them.

StateCore questionIllustrative distinction
IdentityWhat does the person or group call the arrangement?Polyamorous identity can remain meaningful during a currently monogamous period.
PreferenceWhat would participants ideally or realistically want?A person may prefer romantic openness but not sexual openness without emotional connection.
AgreementWhat has been negotiated, permitted, expected, or prohibited?Different partners may believe they agreed to different rules.
EnactmentWhat is actually happening?An agreement may permit outside partners even when no outside relationship currently exists.
ExperienceHow do participants experience the arrangement?The same structure can feel secure to one participant and burdensome to another.
CapacityWhat can participants sustain with available resources?Desire for more relationships may exceed available time, energy, money, or care capacity.
ConstraintWhat is limited by internal or external conditions?Housing, caregiving, stigma, distance, health, employment, or law can narrow what is safely possible.

Original testable constructs

Where alignment, power, and capacity become measurable questions.

≋

Agreement concordance

The degree to which participants share the same understanding of permissions, expectations, obligations, and boundaries.

↔

Preferred–agreed alignment

How closely a person's preferences correspond to the relationship agreement they have actually accepted.

◎

Governance centralization

How much decision-making authority is concentrated in one person, dyad, or household.

⌂

Entanglement concentration

Whether housing, finances, caregiving, social recognition, and legal ties are distributed or concentrated.

⇧

Evolution range

Which future developments—romance, commitment, cohabitation, family, closure, or de-escalation—are possible or prohibited.

⚖

Capacity–commitment fit

Whether time, energy, money, care, health, and emotional resources can sustain the commitments participants have made.

Ethical boundaries

SCOPE does not prescribe one ideal relationship structure.

Monogamy is not less developed. SCOPE can describe questioning, monogamous, monogamish, open, polyamorous, and other arrangements without treating one as evolutionary progress.
Nonhierarchy is not automatically equitable. Stated philosophy must be distinguished from actual control of resources, information, time, and exit options.
Hierarchy is not automatically abusive. Priority and entanglement require context; risk signals should prompt inquiry, not automated moral judgment.
Compersion is not mandatory. Jealousy, happiness, arousal, insecurity, and ambivalence can coexist and should be assessed separately.
