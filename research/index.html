Validation roadmap

Complexity is justified only when it produces better description, explanation, or prediction.

The research program must test the frameworks against simpler alternatives rather than assuming comprehensiveness is automatically superior.

1

Construct definition and expert review

Operationalize each construct; review with sexuality researchers, psychometricians, clinicians, sexual-health professionals, community educators, and people with lived experience.

2

Cognitive interviewing

Test whether respondents actually distinguish identity from attraction, fantasy from intention, openness from enactment, consent from compliance, and hierarchy from entanglement.

3

Pilot item pools and adaptive branching

Begin with broad item pools, remove redundancy, evaluate respondent burden, and preserve “not applicable,” “uncertain,” “prefer not to answer,” and skip options.

4

Structural and profile analysis

Use factor analysis only where latent scales are appropriate; supplement with item-response models, latent-profile analysis, dyadic models, multilevel models, and social-network analysis.

5

Reliability and sensitivity to change

Assess internal consistency where relevant, test–retest stability, branching consistency, interpartner agreement, network reporting consistency, and sensitivity to genuine developmental change.

6

Convergent, discriminant, and incremental validity

Compare with established measures while demonstrating that the frameworks preserve distinctions—such as attraction not being conduct—and add value beyond conventional labels.

7

Measurement invariance and cultural adaptation

Test across gender, sexual identity, race and ethnicity, age, disability, relationship structure, experience, socioeconomic status, language, and national culture.

8

Longitudinal and event-level study

Measure change across identity development, relationship transitions, opening or closing agreements, new-partner formation, triadic events, breakups, caregiving changes, and network reorganization.

Known limitations

What the current models cannot establish.

No integrated psychometric validation

The complete MOSAIC, TRIAD, and SCOPE architectures have not yet demonstrated reliability, factor structure, predictive validity, or measurement invariance as unified instruments.

Self-report cannot independently establish safety

No score can prove consent quality, coercion, fairness, adherence to agreements, relationship health, or the safety of acting on a fantasy.

Research samples may not generalize

Sexuality and CNM research often relies on Western, educated, internet-connected, community-identified, or otherwise nonrepresentative samples.

Some constructs are context-specific

Attraction, role preference, openness, capacity, jealousy, and desired relationship development may differ by person, partner, configuration, event, culture, and life stage.

Comprehensiveness creates burden

Large item banks risk fatigue, careless responding, excessive sensitivity, and false precision. Adaptive branching and modular administration are necessary.

More detail is not automatically more valid

Novel distinctions must demonstrate practical and empirical utility. Constructs that cannot be reliably distinguished should be revised, combined, or removed.

Ethical use

Assessment should support self-understanding without replacing agency.

User-selected identities retain authority. An algorithm may describe patterns or mismatches; it must not overwrite a person’s identity label or assign one they reject.
No pathologizing of variation. Asexuality, aromanticism, fluidity, kink, consensual nonmonogamy, monogamy, jealousy, ambivalence, or uncertainty should not be treated as deficits by default.
No global sexuality or relationship-quality score. Outputs should remain dimensional and explain exactly what each value represents.
No inference from fantasy to intent. Fantasy, curiosity, willingness, intention, agreement, enactment, and experience must remain distinct.
No automated declarations of abuse or readiness. Potential power or consent concerns should prompt contextual inquiry and appropriate support—not unsupported adjudication.
AI narration must be subordinate to deterministic results. AI may explain or rephrase user-authorized data; it must not silently alter answers, scores, or provenance.

Selected conceptual foundations

Research traditions informing the suite.

This website is a public-facing overview rather than a complete bibliography. Full papers should retain formal references and source-level claim tracing.

Multidimensional sexual orientation
Identity, attraction, behavior, romantic attraction, temporal change, and sexual fluidity are not interchangeable constructs.
Sexual script theory
Cultural scenarios, interpersonal expectations, and intrapsychic meanings shape how desire, roles, and acceptable behavior are imagined.
Consent and communication
Consent must be specific, relational, revisable, and distinguished from mere compliance or a broad relationship label.
CNM and relationship diversity
Broad labels do not fully describe relationship structure, governance, openness, priority, autonomy, metamour relations, or entanglement.
Psychometric best practice
Item development should proceed through construct definition, expert review, cognitive interviewing, pilot testing, structural analysis, reliability, validity, invariance, and longitudinal study.
